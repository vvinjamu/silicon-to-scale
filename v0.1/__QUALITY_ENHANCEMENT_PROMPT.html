The Book's Central Mission (Understand This First)

Who This Book Grows The reader starts as a strong software or hardware engineer who knows their current domain well. The book guides them — in 2–3 years of deliberate study and practice — to become a Principal Performance Architect who can design, optimize, and operate AI infrastructure from GPU silicon physics all the way to 100,000-GPU cluster operations. At each level they learn to save infrastructure cost, reduce operational overhead, and increase team productivity through better systems thinking.

Every chapter must serve three readers simultaneously:

  • Foundational reader — a strong software engineer or CS graduate entering AI infrastructure. Has Python and systems basics. Needs mental models built from first principles.
  • Intermediate reader — a senior engineer with 3–5 years in cloud, HPC, or ML engineering. Has seen GPU clusters. Needs to close depth gaps and connect concepts across layers.
  • Principal-level reader — a staff/principal engineer preparing for or operating at the highest technical level. Needs quantitative rigor, cross-system reasoning, and the ability to drive architectural decisions with cost and performance data.

What the Output Should Look Like — Example Excerpts

Example: Key Takeaways Block (Ch 4 — Memory Hierarchy)

Key Takeaways

Core Concepts
  • Memory bandwidth — not compute FLOPS — is the primary bottleneck for most LLM operations. The roofline ridge point for H100 (295 FLOP/byte) means any operation with AI below 295 is memory-bound regardless of how many tensor cores are available.
  • FlashAttention reduces attention memory from O(N²) to O(N) by tiling computation into SRAM and never writing the full score matrix to HBM — enabling 4–8× longer context windows on the same hardware.
  • ECC protection on HBM costs 6.25% of capacity but is non-negotiable in production: a single uncorrectable bit error (Xid 63) requires immediate node replacement to prevent silent model corruption.
Cost & Productivity Impact
  • FlashAttention enables training at 128K context (vs 8K without) on the same H100 cluster — expanding model capability without hardware cost increase. Organizations that adopted it in 2022 saved an estimated 2–4× in GPU-hours for long-context fine-tuning.
  • Proper memory access pattern optimization (coalesced vs strided) alone can improve kernel throughput 3–5×, directly reducing inference cost per token for bandwidth-bound decode operations.
Principal-Level Insight
  • The roofline ridge point shifts with every GPU generation: A100 at 156, H100 at 295, B200 at 563 FLOP/byte. An operation that was memory-bound on A100 may become compute-bound on B200 — your optimization strategy must be re-evaluated when you change hardware generations, not just carried forward.
  • HBM capacity is the real scaling limit for inference, not FLOPS. A 70B model in BF16 requires 140 GB — fitting it on two H100s (80 GB each) requires tensor parallelism that adds AllReduce overhead on every forward pass. Every GB of HBM you save through quantization or GQA directly reduces communication overhead, not just memory cost.

Example: Three-Tier Review Questions (Ch 4 — Memory Hierarchy)

🟢 FOUNDATIONAL — Build the mental model

Q1. A colleague says "our GPU has 989 TFLOPS, so it can do 989 trillion operations per second no matter what we run." Explain why this is wrong using the roofline model. What information is missing from their analysis?


Q2. In plain terms, explain what FlashAttention does differently from standard attention. Why does writing intermediate results to HBM matter for performance? Use an analogy to explain the tiling concept to someone who has never seen a GPU.

🟡 INTERMEDIATE — Apply and calculate

Q3. You are running a GEMV operation (matrix-vector multiply) on H100 with M=1, K=8192, N=8192, BF16. (a) Calculate the arithmetic intensity. (b) Locate it on the H100 roofline. (c) What is the theoretical maximum throughput for this operation? (d) If your measured throughput is 60% of that, what are the two most likely root causes?


Q4. Your production cluster runs LLaMA-3 70B inference with 4K context length. The KV cache is stored in FP16. (a) Calculate KV cache memory per request (L=80, H_kv=8, D=128). (b) If you switch to FP8 KV cache, how many more concurrent requests can you serve on one H100 80 GB? (c) What accuracy risk do you accept and how would you validate it?

🟣 PRINCIPAL LEVEL — Design, decide, and defend

Q5. You are a Principal Architect at a company about to upgrade from H100 to B200 for LLM inference. The B200 has 2.4× the FLOPS but only 2.4× the HBM bandwidth. Your current H100 inference workload runs at 35% MFU on decode (memory-bound). (a) Calculate the expected MFU on B200 for the same workload. (b) Identify which operations will shift performance regime (memory-bound → compute-bound) on B200. (c) Which optimization investments you made for H100 are now less valuable, and where should you redirect engineering effort? Justify every decision quantitatively.


Q6. Your team is evaluating whether to enable FlashAttention-3 for a production 70B model serving 100K requests/day at 8K average context length. You need to present a cost-benefit analysis to your VP. Calculate: (a) current HBM memory used for attention scores per request, (b) throughput improvement expected from FA-3 (use data from the chapter), (c) annual compute cost savings at $2.50/GPU-hr, (d) the engineering investment to validate FA-3 on your serving stack, and (e) your recommendation with explicit assumptions labeled.

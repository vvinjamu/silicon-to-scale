GPU Architecture: From Silicon to FLOPS

How modern accelerators actually work — and how architecture shapes every design choice
Book: AI/ML Infrastructure from Silicon to Scale  |  Author: Venkat Vinjam  |  Cross-ref: Ch 3A: Accelerator landscape Ch 3B: GPU roadmap Ch 4: HBM deep dive Ch 5: Thermal/TCO
"The GPU is not a magic compute box. It is a collection of physical tradeoffs — transistor density, wire length, heat dissipation, memory bandwidth — that determine, with mathematical precision, exactly how fast your model can go and why it cannot go faster." — Principal Performance Architect's GPU Principle

This chapter builds the mental model of the GPU that every subsequent chapter depends on. Chapters 3A and 3B go deeper into the accelerator landscape and specific GPU generations respectively. This chapter covers the architectural concepts that are stable across generations — the compute hierarchy, tensor cores, the memory subsystem, and the transformer engine — so you have the vocabulary and mental model to reason about any GPU, current or future.

3.4 The Transformer Engine (H100 Innovation)

THE TRANSFORMER ENGINE — the key H100 architectural differentiator: Problem: FP16/BF16 is accurate but has 2× the bytes of FP8. FP8 is fast but requires per-tensor scaling to prevent overflow. Solution: Hardware unit that: 1. Monitors activation magnitudes per tensor (automatic, no user code) 2. Computes per-tensor scale factors dynamically 3. Casts to FP8 E4M3 (weights) or FP8 E5M2 (gradients) for GEMM 4. Accumulates in FP32 (prevents numerical degradation) 5. Outputs in BF16 for next layer FP8 E4M3: 1 sign + 4 exponent + 3 mantissa bits → range ±448, more precision FP8 E5M2: 1 sign + 5 exponent + 2 mantissa bits → range ±57344, more range RESULT: ~1.9× GEMM throughput over BF16 with automatic quality preservation. This is the primary reason H100 delivers ~3× training speedup over A100 (FP8 TC throughput × NVLink BW improvement × architectural improvements). FP8 on H100 [SHIPPED]: 1,979 TFLOPS (2× BF16 989 TFLOPS) NVFP4 on B200 [SHIPPED]: 9,000 TFLOPS (FP4 doubles over FP8)

3.6 SXM vs PCIe: The NVLink Question

SXM vs PCIe FORM FACTOR: SXM (Server eXchange Module): Direct die-to-die connection to NVSwitch via copper traces (no PCIe) NVLink BW: 900 GB/s (H100 SXM) vs 400 GB/s (H100 PCIe) TDP: 700W (SXM) vs 350W (PCIe) — SXM requires liquid or active air Deployment: In HGX/DGX/OVX servers — not in standard PCIe servers PCIe: Standard GPU card with PCIe x16 host interface Half the NVLink bandwidth → TP communication is 2× slower Lower power envelope → fits in standard datacenter racks WHEN SXM MATTERS: TP=2 or TP=4 within a node: 900 vs 400 GB/s (SXM 2.25× faster) A 70B model at TP=2 with one AllReduce per layer (80 layers, ~0.5 GB each): SXM: 0.5 GB / 900 GB/s = 0.56 ms overhead per layer PCIe: 0.5 GB / 400 GB/s = 1.25 ms overhead per layer Difference: 0.69 ms × 80 layers = 55 ms per sequence → significant at short output WHEN PCIe IS ACCEPTABLE: DP-only (no TP/PP): no NVLink communication during inference Small models fitting on single GPU: no inter-GPU communication needed

Key Takeaways

Core Concepts
  • The GPU is organized as: GPU → SMs → Warps → Threads. Tensor Cores operate on 16×16×16 tiles; SM occupancy is limited by register pressure (65K regs per SM). At 128 regs/thread, max 512 threads → 25% occupancy. Register spilling adds 200-800× latency per spilled value.
  • The Transformer Engine (H100) automatically manages FP8 ↔ BF16 casting per tensor — no user code required. This delivers 1.9× GEMM throughput over BF16 and is the primary driver of H100's 3× improvement over A100 for transformer workloads.
  • SXM and PCIe are the same compute die with different host interfaces. SXM provides full NVLink bandwidth (900 GB/s H100). PCIe provides half (400 GB/s). For TP=2 inference on 70B, SXM saves ~55ms per sequence vs PCIe — significant for latency-sensitive applications.
Cost & Productivity Impact
  • MI300X's 192 GB HBM3 eliminates the need for TP=2 to serve LLaMA-3 70B (140 GB BF16), saving one GPU per serving instance. At 100 concurrent serving deployments: $2.5M capex savings and $1.3M/year OpEx reduction — purely from fitting the model on fewer GPUs.
  • H100 → H200 upgrade provides zero compute improvement (same TFLOPS) but 43% bandwidth improvement (3.35 → 4.8 TB/s). ROI is positive only for bandwidth-bound workloads: long-context inference, decode at small batches, models that barely fit in memory. Zero ROI for compute-bound prefill workloads.
  • Tensor Core alignment requirements (dimensions must be multiples of 16) are a 'silent tax'. A model with d_head=130 instead of 128 wastes 1.5% of Tensor Core throughput. At 1,000 GPUs running 24/7 at $3/GPU-hr: $39K/year wasted on misalignment alone.
Principal-Level Insight
  • The H100 ridge point (295 FLOP/byte) represents the regime boundary — not a target. Principal architects design workloads to land on the right side of the ridge for their optimization goal: compute-side for throughput (prefill), memory-side for latency (decode). The same model crosses sides at different batch sizes, requiring different optimization strategies.
  • Custom accelerator evaluation requires answering three questions before building a business case: (1) What fraction of your operations are compatible with the accelerator's ISA? (2) What is the team migration cost in engineer-months? (3) At what GPU-spend breakeven does the custom accelerator's per-TFLOP cost advantage amortize the engineering investment? The answer to (3) is typically >$10M/year GPU spend.
  • GPU generation transitions change the regime classification of workloads. An operation at AI=200 FLOP/byte is compute-bound on V100 (ridge 124), near-ridge on H200 (ridge 206), and memory-bound on H100 (ridge 295). When your fleet upgrades GPUs, re-evaluate every kernel's regime classification — optimizations valid for V100 may be wrong on H100.

Review Questions

FoundationalBuild the mental model — no math required
  1. Explain what an SM is and how the relationship between SM count, warp count, and register pressure affects GPU occupancy. Why does low occupancy not always mean low performance?
  2. What is the Transformer Engine in H100, and why does it enable FP8 training without requiring manual scaling from the user? What does it actually do at the hardware level?
  3. Why does the MI300X's 192 GB HBM matter more for inference than for training? What specific serving scenario does it enable that H100 cannot serve without multi-GPU setup?
IntermediateApply and calculate — production context
  1. An H100 cluster is running LLaMA-3 70B training with TP=8. Each AllReduce communicates a tensor of 256 MB (one weight matrix gradient). (a) Calculate the time for this AllReduce over NVLink (900 GB/s, ring algorithm). (b) If the GEMM for that layer takes 8 ms, what is the communication overhead as a percentage? (c) At what tensor size does the AllReduce become the dominant cost?
  2. A kernel uses 128 registers per thread on H100. (a) What is the maximum SM occupancy? (b) If you reduce to 64 registers by restructuring the kernel (without changing FLOP count), what happens to occupancy and why might throughput increase even if the kernel does more L2 accesses? (c) What Nsight Compute metric would you use to diagnose this?
  3. Compare TPOT for LLaMA-3 70B BF16 at batch=1 on H100 (80 GB, 3.35 TB/s), H200 (141 GB, 4.8 TB/s), and MI300X (192 GB, 5.3 TB/s). Which GPU is fastest, and by how much? Does the ridge point change matter for batch=1?

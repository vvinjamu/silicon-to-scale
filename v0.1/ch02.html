Transformer Architecture: A Performance Engineer's Deep Dive

Attention, normalization, positional encoding — seen as memory and compute problems
Book: AI/ML Infrastructure from Silicon to Scale  |  Author: Venkat Vinjam  |  Cross-ref: Ch 4: FlashAttention Ch 6: KV cache serving Ch 11: KV paging
"The transformer is not an algorithm. It is a data movement problem wearing an algorithm's clothes. Every optimization in LLM infrastructure is an optimization for how we move Q, K, V, and the weight matrices through the memory hierarchy." — Performance Engineering Perspective

A performance engineer who cannot derive the FLOP and memory budget of a transformer forward pass from the model config cannot make informed decisions about hardware, batching strategy, parallelism, or quantization. This chapter gives you that derivation — every formula derived from first principles, verified with LLaMA-3 70B numbers that you can check against the published model card.

Learning Objectives

  • Level 1: Explain what attention is doing computationally and why transformers replaced RNNs for language modeling
  • Level 2: Compute exact FLOPs and memory for any transformer configuration; classify each operation by arithmetic intensity and regime
  • Level 3: Reason about architecture choices (GQA vs MHA, MLA vs GQA, MoE sparsity) as infrastructure engineering decisions with quantified cost consequences

2.1 Why Performance Engineers Must Understand Transformers

Every GPU workload in modern AI is a transformer workload. The model config — layers, hidden dim, heads, FFN multiplier, vocab size, sequence length, attention variant — directly and quantifiably determines hardware requirements. You cannot right-size a cluster, choose a quantization format, set a tensor parallelism degree, or engineer an SLA without being able to derive these numbers from first principles.

What you must be able to derive from a model card: (1) total parameter count and memory in any precision, (2) FLOPs per token for prefill vs decode, (3) KV cache bytes per token per request, (4) arithmetic intensity of each operation class, (5) which operations are compute- vs memory-bound at each batch size — before running a single experiment.

2.2 Attention as Matrix Multiplication: The FLOP and Memory Breakdown

Scaled dot-product attention computes: Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QKT / √d_k) × V. For a performance engineer, this is two matrix multiplies (QKT and Attn×V) separated by a softmax with specific memory access patterns.

ATTENTION FLOP ANALYSIS — single head, single layer: S = sequence length, d = head dimension = d_model / H_q QK^T product: 2 × S × d × S = 2S²d FLOPs (outer product) Softmax: ~5 × S² FLOPs (≈1% of total — negligible vs GEMM) AV product: 2 × S × S × d = 2S²d FLOPs Total per head: 4S²d FLOPs (ignoring softmax) HBM memory traffic (standard attention, BF16): Reads: Q(S×d) + K(S×d) + P(S×S) + V(S×d) = 4Sd + S² × 2 bytes Writes: S(S×S) + P(S×S) + O(S×d) = 2S² + Sd × 2 bytes Total: (4Sd + 3S²) × 2 bytes ← the S² term is what kills standard attention Arithmetic Intensity (per head, S=512, d=128): AI = 4S²d / ((4Sd + 3S²) × 2) ≈ 4S²d / (6S² × 2) = d/3 = 43 FLOP/byte → Memory-bound on H100 (ridge = 295) At S=4096: AI ≈ d/3 = 43 FLOP/byte (still memory-bound — S cancels!) Key insight: standard attention is always memory-bound due to the N² matrix. FlashAttention eliminates the N² HBM traffic entirely. (→ Ch 4 §4.8)

2.3 Multi-Head Attention: Parallelism and KV Cache

MULTI-HEAD ATTENTION QKV PROJECTION FLOPs (LLaMA-3 70B): Config: d_model=8192, H_q=64 heads, H_kv=8 (GQA), d_head=128 Q projection: 2 × d_model × H_q × d_head = 2 × 8192 × 64 × 128 = 134.2M FLOPs K projection: 2 × d_model × H_kv × d_head = 2 × 8192 × 8 × 128 = 16.8M FLOPs V projection: same as K = 16.8M FLOPs Output proj: 2 × d_model × H_q × d_head = 2 × 8192 × 8192 = 134.2M FLOPs Total QKV+O: 302.0M FLOPs per token per layer KV CACHE MEMORY (the serving cost that persists across tokens): Per token per layer (BF16): 2 (K+V) × H_kv × d_head × 2 bytes = 2 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 4,096 bytes = 4 KB/token/layer Full model (L=80 layers, S=4096 ctx, BF16): KV_bytes = 2 × L × H_kv × d_head × S × 2 = 2 × 80 × 8 × 128 × 4096 × 2 = 5.37 GB per request On H100 (80 GB HBM), 10 GB for weights, 10 GB for activations: Available for KV: 60 GB / 5.37 GB = ~11 concurrent 4K requests (BF16) INT8 KV: 2.7 GB/request → 22 concurrent requests (2× more serving capacity)

2.5 RMSNorm vs. LayerNorm

LAYERNORM vs. RMSNORM: LayerNorm: y = ((x - μ) / σ) × γ + β μ = mean(x), σ = std(x) — requires 2 passes over data FLOPs/element: ~8 (mean, variance, subtract, divide, scale, shift) Memory AI: ~1.5 FLOP/byte → memory-bound RMSNorm (LLaMA, Mistral, Qwen — modern standard): y = x / RMS(x) × γ, where RMS(x) = √(mean(x²)) FLOPs/element: ~5 (square, mean, sqrt, divide, scale) — no mean subtraction Memory AI: ~1.25 FLOP/byte → memory-bound WHY RMSNorm won in production: 1. 40% fewer FLOPs — measurable at 80-layer models, 10K tokens/s serving 2. Simpler gradient: no mean subtraction → cleaner backward pass 3. Empirically: negligible quality difference on modern LLMs at scale PRODUCTION IMPLICATION: Both are deeply memory-bound (AI < 2). Standalone, they waste 99%+ of compute. Solution: ALWAYS fuse with adjacent GEMM or elementwise op. Unfused RMSNorm at 10B tokens/day costs ~12 GB/s of wasted HBM BW — trivial individually but adds up across 80 layers × 10,000 requests/sec.

2.6 Positional Encoding: RoPE and Context Length

RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) encodes position by rotating Q and K vectors in pairs of dimensions. The rotation matrix R_m for position m is applied as Q_m = R_m × Q, K_n = R_n × K. The inner product QKT then depends only on relative position (m−n), giving the model positional information without absolute embeddings. YaRN and LongRoPE extend the effective context window by 2–4× beyond training length by modifying the interpolation of rotation frequencies.

2.7 Feed-Forward Networks and the Activation Bottleneck

FFN FLOP COMPARISON (LLaMA-3 70B, d_model=8192, d_ffn=28,672): Standard FFN (GPT-style): FFN(x) = max(0, xW₁ + b₁)W₂ + b₂ W₁: [d_model × d_ffn], W₂: [d_ffn × d_model] FLOPs: 2 × 2 × S × d_model × d_ffn = 2 × 2 × S × 8192 × 32768 ≈ not used in LLaMA SwiGLU FFN (LLaMA-3 standard): FFN(x) = (SiLU(xW_gate) ⊗ xW_up) × W_down Three matrices: W_gate, W_up ∈ [d_model × d_ffn], W_down ∈ [d_ffn × d_model] FLOPs: 3 × 2 × S × d_model × d_ffn = 3 × 2 × S × 8192 × 28,672 Per token per layer: FFN FLOPs = 3 × 2 × 1 × 8192 × 28,672 = 1,409 M FLOPs [SHIPPED derivation] QKV+O FLOPs (from §2.3) = 302 M FLOPs Attention score FLOPs (at S=1): = 0.13 M FLOPs (negligible) FFN fraction at decode S=1: 1409 / (1409 + 302) = 82.4% FFN fraction at prefill S=4096: 1409 / (1409 + 302 + 67) = 79% → The FFN is the dominant compute consumer at decode (83%) and prefill (79%). Quantizing FFN weights delivers the highest bandwidth reduction per bit. INT4 FFN: 4× less weight bytes → 4× faster decode (bandwidth-bound).

2.8 Complete FLOP Budget — LLaMA-3 70B

FULL TRANSFORMER LAYER FLOP FORMULA: Per-layer FLOPs = QKV_proj + Attn_score + Output_proj + FFN LLaMA-3 70B per token per layer: Q proj: 134.2 M FLOPs (2 × 8192 × 8192) K proj: 16.8 M FLOPs (2 × 8192 × 1024, GQA H_kv=8) V proj: 16.8 M FLOPs Attn score (S=1): 0.13M FLOPs (negligible at decode) Output proj: 134.2 M FLOPs FFN SwiGLU: 1,409.3 M FLOPs (3 × 2 × 8192 × 28672) ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────── Total/layer: 1,711.4 M FLOPs ≈ 1.71 GFLOP per token per layer Total (L=80): 80 × 1.71 = 136.9 GFLOP per token (full forward pass) TPOT lower bound (batch=1, H100 989 TFLOPS, 100% MFU): 136.9 GFLOP / 989,000 GFLOP/s = 0.138 ms minimum (compute floor) BUT: model weights = 140 GB BF16, bandwidth = 3.35 TB/s Loading time: 140 GB / 3.35 TB/s = 41.8 ms minimum (bandwidth floor!) The bandwidth floor (41.8ms) is 303× higher than the compute floor (0.138ms). Decode is never compute-limited. It is always bandwidth-limited at any batch size below the arithmetic intensity ridge point (batch ≈ 295 for H100).

2.10 Modern Variants: GQA, MQA, MLA, MoE

KV CACHE REDUCTION VARIANTS: MHA (Multi-Head Attention, baseline): H_q query heads, H_kv = H_q key-value heads KV bytes/token/layer = 2 × H_kv × d_head × 2 = 2 × 64 × 128 × 2 = 32,768 bytes GQA (Grouped-Query Attention, LLaMA-3 standard): H_q = 64, H_kv = 8 (GQA ratio R = 8) KV bytes/token/layer = 2 × 8 × 128 × 2 = 4,096 bytes ← 8× reduction Quality loss: ~0% (empirically verified at 70B+ scale) Production use: LLaMA-3 70B, Mistral, Gemma, Qwen, Falcon MQA (Multi-Query Attention, extreme): H_q = 64, H_kv = 1 KV bytes/token/layer = 2 × 1 × 128 × 2 = 512 bytes ← 64× reduction Quality loss: ~1–2% on complex reasoning tasks MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention, DeepSeek): Compress KV to low-rank latent c_KV ∈ [d_c] (d_c << H_q × d_head) DeepSeek-V3: d_c=512, d_model=7168, H_q=128 KV bytes/token/layer ≈ d_c × 2 = 1,024 bytes ← 32× reduction vs MHA Reconstruction: K,V = c_KV × W_UK, W_UV (GEMM at decode — compute cost) MoE (Mixture-of-Experts): N expert FFNs, route each token to K of N (K << N) Mixtral 8×7B: N=8, K=2, d_ffn=14336 per expert Active FLOPs per token: K/N × N × FFN_flops = 2/8 × total = same as dense! Total params: N × FFN + shared = 8 × 7B = 47B params (vs 7B active) Key system cost: All-to-All collective for expert routing (Ch 13)

Key Takeaways

Core Concepts
  • The FFN consumes 83% of FLOPs at decode (batch=1) and 79% at prefill. This is why FFN weight quantization (INT4/FP8) has the highest bandwidth ROI of any quantization decision.
  • KV cache size = 2 × L × H_kv × d_head × S × dtype_bytes. For LLaMA-3 70B at 4K context in BF16: 5.37 GB per concurrent request. GQA (H_kv=8 vs H_q=64) delivers an 8× reduction with negligible quality loss — directly 8× more concurrent users per GPU.
  • Decode batch=1 TPOT is always bandwidth-limited, never compute-limited. The H100 bandwidth floor (41.8ms for 70B BF16) is 303× higher than the compute floor (0.14ms). No kernel optimization can help — only quantization (reducing bytes) or batching (increasing arithmetic intensity).
Cost & Productivity Impact
  • Switching from MHA to GQA (H_kv: 64 → 8) on a 70B model reduces KV cache from 42 GB to 5.4 GB at 4K context. On an 80 GB H100 with 10 GB overhead, this increases max concurrent requests from ~1 to ~11 per GPU — an 11× serving capacity gain.
  • INT4 quantization of the FFN weights (70B BF16 → 35 GB) reduces the per-token weight loading from 140 GB to 35 GB, directly delivering 4× TPOT improvement (41.8ms → 10.4ms) at batch=1. The bandwidth floor is the only floor that matters.
  • MLA (DeepSeek) reduces KV cache by 32× over MHA but introduces a GEMM at decode time (reconstructing K,V from the latent). This GEMM is compute-bound at batch=1 — adding a new compute cost in exchange for bandwidth savings. The tradeoff breaks even at batch>8 approximately.
Principal-Level Insight
  • Every architecture choice is an infrastructure engineering decision: MHA vs GQA vs MLA determines serving capacity per GPU. The model team's quality preference must be evaluated against the serving team's GPU cost. A 1% quality improvement that requires 4× more GPUs costs $100K/year in GPU time on a 100-GPU serving cluster.
  • MoE routing introduces an All-to-All collective at every FFN layer — absent in dense models. At 8 experts with top-2 routing, 75% of expert compute happens remotely (off the local GPU). The communication overhead of this All-to-All determines whether MoE saves cost or adds it — see Ch 13.
  • The quadratic crossover (attention FLOPs exceeding FFN FLOPs) for LLaMA-3 70B occurs at S ≈ 65,000 tokens. Below this, FFN dominates. Only long-context inference (RAG, document processing, code repositories) enters the attention-dominated regime — design your hardware and batching strategy accordingly.

Cluster Management & Orchestration

SLURM, Kubernetes, Gang Scheduling, and the Art of Operating 10,000-GPU Clusters
Chapter 16 ~42 pages Prerequisite: Ch 14 (Networking), Ch 15 (Storage) Next: Ch 17 (Telemetry)
"A 10,000-GPU cluster running at 60% utilization costs the same as one running at 85% utilization. The difference is the scheduler. Getting scheduling right is worth more than the next GPU generation." — Production AI Infrastructure Maxim

A Principal Performance Architect who can design the perfect GPU kernel but cannot schedule, monitor, and operate a GPU cluster at scale is only half-equipped. The orchestration layer — the software that decides which workload runs on which GPU, when, and in what configuration — is where the economics of AI infrastructure are won or lost. A poorly-configured scheduler on a 1,000-GPU cluster at $3/GPU-hr wastes $12,600 per day for every 10% of avoidable idle time. This chapter provides a complete, hands-on guide to the two dominant cluster management systems in AI infrastructure: SLURM for large-scale HPC and training, and Kubernetes for cloud-native serving and multi-tenant platforms.

Learning Objectives by Level

  • Foundational: Understand why cluster schedulers exist, what SLURM and Kubernetes do, and how distributed training jobs are different from normal batch jobs.
  • Intermediate: Write production SLURM job scripts, configure QOS/fair-share policies, deploy GPU workloads on Kubernetes, and diagnose pending-job failures.
  • Principal: Design multi-tenant scheduling architectures, reason about MTBF/cost tradeoffs, choose between SLURM and Kubernetes for any given workload, and build fault-tolerant 1,000+ GPU training pipelines with quantified failure-recovery economics.

16.1 The Cluster Management Problem: Utilization, Fairness, and Efficiency

Why do we need specialized cluster schedulers at all? Consider a 512-GPU cluster with 20 teams submitting jobs. Without a scheduler, every team would SSH to nodes directly, GPU contention would be resolved by whoever got there first, and there would be no accounting of who used what. With a scheduler, the cluster becomes a shared resource with policies: fairness, priority, topology placement, accounting, and fault recovery are all encoded as configuration rather than convention.

The economics are stark. An H100 SXM node with 8 GPUs costs approximately $300,000 capital. At $0.40/GPU-hr (amortized TCO for on-premise), a 512-GPU cluster costs $204 per hour to operate. Improving cluster utilization from 65% to 85% recovers $40.8/hr — over $357,000 per year from scheduling improvements alone, without purchasing a single additional GPU. (See Chapter 5 for full TCO derivations.)

16.1.1 The Three Scheduling Problems

Cluster management for AI workloads involves three distinct, sometimes conflicting, optimization problems:

Problem 1: Gang Scheduling (All-or-Nothing) A 512-GPU distributed training job requires ALL 512 GPUs to start simultaneously. If even one is unavailable, the entire job stalls at the NCCL initialization barrier. Unlike traditional HPC, where a job can start with partial resources and grow, LLM training requires perfect collective synchronization from step 0. The scheduler must either allocate all requested resources or allocate none — this is the "gang" constraint.
Problem 2: Topology Awareness Not all GPUs are equal from a communication perspective. Two GPUs connected by NVLink (900 GB/s) can AllReduce a 1 GB gradient in 2.2 ms. Two GPUs communicating via InfiniBand NDR across two spine hops need ~0.8 ms per message, making large AllReduces 5–10× slower. The scheduler must place tensor-parallel groups within a single node (NVLink domain) and pipeline-parallel stages on the same ToR switch — or pay the bandwidth penalty in every training step for the entire run.
Problem 3: Multi-Tenancy and Fairness A research cluster serves multiple teams with different workload types: a team running a 7-day 1,024-GPU pre-training job (high priority, needs guaranteed allocation), a team running hyperparameter search (medium priority, many small jobs, tolerates preemption), and a team running CI/CD evaluation (low priority, short jobs, needs fast queue turnaround). The scheduler must enforce fairness without creating priority inversions, and must preempt lower-priority jobs gracefully when higher-priority ones arrive.

16.3 SLURM Job Scripting for Multi-Node GPU Training

The job script is the contract between the researcher and the scheduler. A well-written script encodes resource requirements precisely, sets up the distributed environment correctly, handles signals for graceful preemption, and always exits with a meaningful code. A poorly written script wastes GPUs, causes NCCL hangs, and leaves zombie processes on nodes.

16.3.1 Production Job Script Anatomy

#!/bin/bash #SBATCH --job-name=llama3-70b-pretrain #SBATCH --nodes=64 # 64 nodes × 8 GPUs = 512 GPUs total #SBATCH --ntasks-per-node=8 # One rank per GPU (standard for PyTorch DDP) #SBATCH --gres=gpu:h100:8 # 8 H100s per node (type-qualified) #SBATCH --cpus-per-task=12 # 12 CPU cores per rank (96 CPUs / 8 GPUs) #SBATCH --mem=240G # 30 GB per rank (240 GB / 8 ranks per node) #SBATCH --time=168:00:00 # 7-day wall time limit #SBATCH --partition=gpu-train # Training partition (not inference) #SBATCH --qos=high-priority # QOS tier — affects scheduling priority #SBATCH --account=team-research # Accounting account for fair-share #SBATCH --output=/checkpoint/slurm/llama3-%j.out #SBATCH --error=/checkpoint/slurm/llama3-%j.err #SBATCH --switches=8@00:30:00 # Prefer ≤8 ToR switches; 16.3.2 SIGTERM Handler for Graceful Preemption

When SLURM preempts a job, it sends SIGTERM to all processes (followed by SIGKILL after the configured grace period). Without a signal handler, the job dies immediately, losing the current checkpoint. With a handler, the job checkpoints at the current step and exits cleanly — SLURM then requeues it to resume from the checkpoint. On a 7-day run, this difference is $4,608 of wasted compute vs. $0 for each preemption event at $3/GPU-hr on 512 GPUs.

# In train.py — SIGTERM/SIGUSR1 handler for SLURM preemption import signal, sys, torch.distributed as dist CHECKPOINT_DIR = "/checkpoint/llama3-70b/" def checkpoint_and_exit(signum, frame): if dist.get_rank() == 0: print(f"[RANK 0] Signal {signum} received — saving checkpoint...") save_checkpoint(model, optimizer, global_step, CHECKPOINT_DIR) dist.barrier() # All ranks wait for rank 0 to finish writing sys.exit(0) # Exit with code 0 → SLURM interprets as clean exit → requeue signal.signal(signal.SIGTERM, checkpoint_and_exit) signal.signal(signal.SIGUSR1, checkpoint_and_exit) # SLURM sends SIGUSR1 before SIGTERM # SLURM requeue config in slurm.conf: # KillWait=120 → 120s between SIGTERM and SIGKILL (checkpoint window) # RequeueExit=0 → Requeue jobs that exit with code 0

16.4 Topology-Aware Scheduling and Gang Scheduling

Topology awareness is the scheduling feature with the highest direct impact on distributed training throughput. Without it, a 512-GPU job scattered across maximum fabric hops can spend 35% of step time in AllReduce instead of 8% — a 27-percentage-point MFU loss that persists for the entire run.

16.4.1 Gang Scheduling for Distributed Training

Gang scheduling ensures all tasks of a distributed job start simultaneously. Without it, Rank 0 might launch and begin NCCL initialization while Rank 127 is still waiting for its allocation — causing a timeout that kills the entire job. SLURM implements gang scheduling natively through srun: it launches all tasks but holds them at a synchronization barrier until all are ready, then releases the entire cohort simultaneously.

SLURM Gang Scheduling — Why the Default Works SLURM's srun command implements all-or-nothing launch semantics: all task slots are allocated before any task receives CPU time. The scheduler won't start a job until ALL nodes in the allocation are available (no partial start). This is different from MPI's non-gang-safe mode where processes can start individually. For NCCL-based training, this is the correct behavior — never configure --ntasks-per-node less than the number of GPUs you intend to use, or you will lose gang semantics.
# Verifying gang start — all ranks should show identical start timestamps sacct -j ${SLURM_JOB_ID} --format=JobID,Start,Elapsed --parsable2 # Example output (correct gang start): # JobID|Start|Elapsed # 12345.0|2024-11-15T14:30:00|00:00:01 # 12345.1|2024-11-15T14:30:00|00:00:01 ← same second as .0 # 12345.2|2024-11-15T14:30:00|00:00:01 ← correct # topology.conf for SLURM topological scheduling SwitchName=spine01 Switches=tor[01-16] SwitchName=spine02 Switches=tor[17-32] SwitchName=tor01 Nodes=gpu[001-016] SwitchName=tor02 Nodes=gpu[017-032] # ... define all switches # Job script: request topology constraint #SBATCH --switches=8@00:30:00 # Up to 8 ToR switches, wait up to 30 min for better placement # For a 64-node job on 8-node ToRs: 8 switches = all nodes within 8 racks # This guarantees max 2-hop IB communication (intra-rail)

16.5 QOS, Fair-Share, and Multi-Tenant Policies

Fair-share scheduling solves the problem of preventing any single team from monopolizing a shared cluster. The algorithm is conceptually simple: track how much each account should have received (normalized share) vs. how much it did receive (usage over a decay window). Accounts that have used less than their share get priority; accounts that have over-consumed get deprioritized. This automatically corrects for bursty usage patterns without requiring manual intervention.

# Fair-share priority formula (SLURM multifactor plugin): Priority = PriorityWeightAge × age_factor + PriorityWeightFairshare × fairshare_factor + PriorityWeightQOS × qos_factor + PriorityWeightJobSize × jobsize_factor # fairshare_factor = 2^(-effective_usage / normalized_share) # If team used 0% of their share → factor = 1.0 (max priority) # If team used 100% of share → factor = 0.5 # If team used 200% of share → factor = 0.25 # Decay half-life (14 days) means old usage counts less over time # ── Setting up a 5-team multi-tenant cluster ────────────────── sacctmgr add cluster gpu-cluster # Create top-level accounts with share allocations sacctmgr add account research Share=40 Description="Research Division" sacctmgr add account inference Share=30 Description="Inference Serving" sacctmgr add account evaluation Share=20 Description="Model Evaluation" sacctmgr add account dev Share=10 Description="Development/CI" # Create users under accounts sacctmgr add user alice Account=research Partition=gpu-train sacctmgr add user bob Account=inference Partition=gpu-infer sacctmgr add user ci_bot Account=evaluation Partition=gpu-eval DefaultQOS=ci # Create QOS tiers with resource limits sacctmgr add qos high-priority MaxWall=336:00:00 Priority=1000 GrpTRES=gpu=512 sacctmgr add qos standard MaxWall=168:00:00 Priority=500 GrpTRES=gpu=256 sacctmgr add qos batch MaxWall=72:00:00 Priority=100 GrpTRES=gpu=64 sacctmgr add qos ci MaxWall=4:00:00 Priority=200 GrpTRES=gpu=16 \ MaxJobsPerUser=5 MaxSubmitJobsPerUser=20 # View fair-share status (sshare shows actual vs. effective usage) sshare -l -u alice,bob # Account User RawShares RawUsage NormShares NormUsage FairShare # research alice 40 2847.2 0.40 0.12 0.97 ← under-utilized, gets boost # inference bob 30 8432.1 0.30 0.52 0.71 ← over-utilized, deprioritized

16.6 Kubernetes for AI — Architecture and GPU Scheduling

Kubernetes has become the dominant platform for AI inference serving, multi-tenant research platforms, and increasingly, large-scale training. Its containerized resource model, rich RBAC, and native integration with cloud auto-scaling make it the right choice wherever workloads look more like microservices than batch HPC jobs.

GPUs enter Kubernetes through the NVIDIA device plugin, which runs as a DaemonSet and advertises GPU resources to the Kubernetes API server. From the scheduler's perspective, GPUs are just another extended resource: nvidia.com/gpu: 8 in a pod spec is equivalent to requesting 8 CPU cores — the scheduler finds a node with 8 available GPUs and places the pod there.

# GPU deployment: PyTorch training pod with resource requests apiVersion: v1 kind: Pod metadata: name: llama-finetune-worker labels: app: llama-finetune task-type: training spec: containers: - name: trainer image: nvcr.io/nvidia/pytorch:24.05-py3 resources: requests: nvidia.com/gpu: "8" # Request 8 GPUs cpu: "96" # Match CPU count to GPUs memory: "512Gi" limits: nvidia.com/gpu: "8" # Limits must equal requests for GPUs cpu: "96" memory: "512Gi" env: - name: NCCL_IB_DISABLE value: "0" - name: NVIDIA_VISIBLE_DEVICES value: all # Device plugin sets CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES tolerations: - key: nvidia.com/gpu operator: Exists effect: NoSchedule # Required: GPU nodes tainted to prevent CPU-only workloads nodeSelector: nvidia.com/gpu.product: "NVIDIA-H100-80GB-HBM3" # Match specific GPU model --- # Multi-tenant namespace isolation with ResourceQuota apiVersion: v1 kind: ResourceQuota metadata: name: research-team-quota namespace: research spec: hard: requests.nvidia.com/gpu: "256" # Max 256 GPUs for research namespace limits.nvidia.com/gpu: "256" requests.cpu: "3072" requests.memory: "12Ti" pods: "500" count/deployments.apps: "50"

16.6.1 Diagnosing Pending GPU Pods

The most common Kubernetes support request is "my GPU pod is stuck in Pending." There are exactly five root causes, and they can be diagnosed in under two minutes with the right command sequence:

# Step 1: Get the exact scheduling failure reason kubectl describe pod <pod-name> -n <namespace> # Look for Events section at the bottom — this is the diagnosis: # Cause 1: Insufficient GPU resources # Events: 0/8 nodes are available: 8 Insufficient nvidia.com/gpu # Fix: Check GPU availability (kubectl describe nodes | grep nvidia.com/gpu) # or reduce request # Cause 2: ResourceQuota exceeded # Events: exceeded quota: research, requested: requests.nvidia.com/gpu=8, # used: requests.nvidia.com/gpu=252, limited: requests.nvidia.com/gpu=256 # Fix: Delete idle pods or increase quota # Cause 3: Affinity/taint mismatch # Events: 0/8 nodes are available: 8 node(s) had untolerated taint # Fix: Add toleration for nvidia.com/gpu NoSchedule # Cause 4: Node selector mismatch # Events: 0/8 nodes are available: 8 node(s) didn't match Pod's node affinity # Fix: kubectl get nodes --show-labels | grep gpu.product # Cause 5: Device plugin not running kubectl get pods -n kube-system | grep device-plugin # If no nvidia-device-plugin DaemonSet pods → GPUs not registered with k8s # Fix: Helm install nvidia-device-plugin or re-register

16.7 Gang Scheduling on Kubernetes: Volcano and Kueue

Kubernetes' default scheduler is not gang-scheduler aware. Without a gang scheduling plugin, a 512-GPU distributed training job (512 pods) can partially start: pods 1–400 might acquire their GPUs while pods 401–512 wait in a resource contention queue. The running pods spin at the NCCL barrier, burning 400 GPUs worth of cost ($1,200/hr) producing zero useful work until the remaining pods are scheduled — or a 10-minute NCCL timeout kills everything. This is why Volcano and Kueue exist.

16.8 Fault Tolerance: MTBF, SDC, and Node Quarantine

At scale, hardware failure is not an exception — it is the steady state. A cluster of 1,024 H100 GPUs has approximately 8,192 individual components (GPU dies, NVLink connections, memory stacks, PCIe lanes). With a mean time between failures (MTBF) of ~100,000 hours per component, the expected time to the first failure across the cluster is just 12 hours. Meta's production Llama 3 training runs on 24,000 GPUs experienced ~50% fewer cluster interruptions after systematically investing in automated failure detection and recovery — demonstrating that engineering the recovery path is as important as engineering the training algorithm.

16.8.1 The Three-Layer Fault Detection Hierarchy

Layer 1: Hardware Telemetry (DCGM) NVIDIA DCGM runs as a service on every GPU node and exposes hardware health metrics at 10-second intervals. Key metrics to monitor: GPU ECC single-bit errors (DCGM_FI_DEV_ECC_SBE_AGG_TOTAL — more than 10/hour predicts imminent failure), temperature (throttle at 83°C, thermal runaway above 87°C), PCIe replay counter (non-zero = signal integrity issue), and NVLink error counter (any errors = degraded bandwidth). See Chapter 17 for complete DCGM metric catalog.
Layer 2: SLURM Prolog/Epilog Health Checks Prolog scripts run on every node before a job starts; epilog scripts run after it ends. A prolog that exits non-zero causes SLURM to mark the node as drained and move the job to the next available set of nodes. This is the first line of defense against placing a training job on a degraded node.
Layer 3: Silent Data Corruption (SDC) Detection SDC is the worst failure mode: the GPU appears healthy (no ECC errors), but produces wrong computation results. SDC manifests as training loss divergence or incorrect model outputs and is notoriously hard to detect. The state-of-the-art approach is to run "golden inputs" tests — pre-computed tensors with known correct outputs — and verify exact match. Meta, Google, and NVIDIA all run SDC screening tests before assigning nodes to production training jobs.
# ── SLURM prolog: node health check (runs before each job) ──────────────── #!/bin/bash # /opt/slurm/prolog.d/01-gpu-health.sh NODE_PASS=1 # Check 1: GPU count (should be 8 on all nodes) GPU_COUNT=$(nvidia-smi --query-gpu=name --format=csv,noheader | wc -l) if [ "$GPU_COUNT" -ne 8 ]; then scontrol update NodeName=${SLURM_JOB_NODELIST} State=DRAIN \ Reason="GPU count mismatch: expected 8, got ${GPU_COUNT}" exit 1 fi # Check 2: ECC error rate (accumulated since last reset) ECC_ERRORS=$(dcgmi dmon -e 160 -c 1 | awk 'NR>1{sum+=$2}END{print sum+0}') if [ "$ECC_ERRORS" -gt 100 ]; then scontrol update NodeName=${SLURM_JOB_NODELIST} State=DRAIN \ Reason="ECC errors exceed threshold: ${ECC_ERRORS} SBE" exit 1 fi # Check 3: NVLink errors (any = fabric degradation) NVLINK_ERR=$(nvidia-smi nvlink -e -i 0 | grep -c "Error Count" || true) if [ "$NVLINK_ERR" -gt 0 ]; then scontrol update NodeName=${SLURM_JOB_NODELIST} State=DRAIN \ Reason="NVLink errors detected" exit 1 fi echo "[PROLOG] GPU health check PASSED on ${SLURM_JOB_NODELIST}" exit 0

16.9 Multi-Tenant GPU Sharing: MIG, MPS, and Time-Slicing

Not every workload needs a full GPU. A CI/CD model evaluation job that runs 1,000 forward passes needs perhaps 2 GB of VRAM and 20% of SM utilization — assigning it a whole H100 wastes 80 GB of HBM and 80% of compute. The three GPU sharing mechanisms — MIG, MPS, and time-slicing — address this at different granularities, with different isolation and performance guarantees.

# MIG configuration on H100 (7× 1g.10gb instances) nvidia-smi mig -cgi 1g.10gb,1g.10gb,1g.10gb,1g.10gb,1g.10gb,1g.10gb,1g.10gb -C # Verify MIG instances nvidia-smi mig -lgi # Expected: 7 GPU instances, each with 1 GPC, 10 GB memory # Kubernetes: expose MIG instances to pods # With MIG strategy="mixed" in device plugin config: resources: limits: nvidia.com/mig-1g.10gb: "1" # Request one 10GB MIG slice # MPS: enable for all processes on GPU 0 export CUDA_MPS_PIPE_DIRECTORY=/tmp/nvidia-mps export CUDA_MPS_LOG_DIRECTORY=/tmp/nvidia-log nvidia-cuda-mps-control -d # Start MPS daemon echo "set_active_thread_percentage 50" | nvidia-cuda-mps-control # Limits each MPS client to 50% of SMs (prevents one job starving others) # K8s time-slicing config (device plugin configmap) version: v1 flags: migStrategy: none sharing: timeSlicing: replicas: 4 # Present each physical GPU as 4 virtual GPUs # Pods can now request nvidia.com/gpu: 1 and share a physical GPU # WARNING: No memory isolation — use only for trusted workloads

16.10 SLURM vs. Kubernetes — Decision Framework

The choice between SLURM and Kubernetes is not a matter of one being better — it's a matter of fit to workload characteristics. The correct answer for most large organizations is both: SLURM for training, Kubernetes for inference, with a lightweight bridge (MLFlow, Kubeflow, Weights & Biases) for experiment tracking across both. Meta's production architecture is precisely this: SLURM-like gang scheduling semantics for physical training clusters, Kubernetes-style container management for the inference serving layer.

Decision Rule for New Clusters If your primary workload is multi-node distributed training (>16 nodes) and you have InfiniBand fabric and a dedicated HPC team: use SLURM. If your primary workload is inference serving, multi-tenant research with container images, or you're deploying on cloud (EKS/GKE/AKS): use Kubernetes. If you have both: use both, with SLURM for training and K8s for serving.

16.11.2 Capacity Planning Formula

Capacity planning for AI clusters requires reasoning from the training roadmap backward to GPU-hours, then forward to fleet size. The key insight: you need to provision for peak demand with N+1 fault tolerance, not average utilization.

# Capacity planning: GPU-hours → fleet size Fleet_GPUs = (Weekly_GPU_Hours_Demand × Safety_Factor) / (168 hrs × Target_Utilization) Example: Team trains 3 models/week, each needing 1M GPU-hours Weekly_demand = 3 × 1,000,000 = 3,000,000 GPU-hours/week Safety_factor = 1.3 # 30% headroom for failures, reruns, experiments Target_util = 0.80 # 80% realistic sustained utilization Hours_per_week = 168 Fleet_GPUs = (3,000,000 × 1.3) / (168 × 0.80) = 29,018 GPUs ≈ 30,000 GPUs # Annual cost at $3/GPU-hr (amortized TCO): Annual_cost = 30,000 × $3 × 8,760 = $789M/year # Impact of utilization improvement (80% → 85%): same output, fewer GPUs Fleet_at_85% = 30,000 × (0.80/0.85) = 28,235 GPUs GPUs_freed = 1,765 → Savings = 1,765 × $3 × 8,760 = $46.4M/year
Worked Example — Fault Tolerance Cost Analysis: 1,024-GPU LLM Pre-Training

Scenario: You're planning a 7-day, 1,024-GPU LLM pre-training job on H100s at $3/GPU-hr. Cluster MTBF is 50 hours per node (8 GPUs per node). Assume no fault tolerance vs. a well-engineered checkpoint/requeue pipeline. Quantify the GPU-hour cost of each approach.

Given:

  • Duration: 7 days = 168 hours
  • GPUs: 1,024 (128 nodes × 8 GPUs)
  • Node MTBF: 50 hours
  • Cost: $3/GPU-hr
  • Checkpoint time: 3 minutes
  • Recovery time (no fault tolerance): full restart from hour 0
  • Recovery time (with fault tolerance): resume from last checkpoint (every 30 min)

Step 1: Expected number of failures

Nodes = 128 Run_duration = 168 hours Expected_failures = Nodes × (Run_duration / MTBF) = 128 × (168 / 50) = 128 × 3.36 = 430 expected failures over the run # But failures follow Poisson: time to FIRST failure is the critical metric Time_to_first_failure = MTBF / Nodes = 50 / 128 = 0.39 hours ≈ 23 minutes # A 1,024-GPU cluster will see its first failure within 23 minutes on average. # Without fault tolerance, a job that restarts from scratch loses ALL prior work.

Step 2: GPU-hours wasted without fault tolerance

# Assume failures are detected and job restarts from hour 0 each time # Poisson process: Nth failure follows Gamma distribution # Simplified: model as sequential → each failure wastes hours since last successful start # In practice, at 0.39 hr between failures, the job can never complete # Without checkpointing: infinite loop (job never reaches hour 168) # Conservative model: assume 1 failure per run at random point (expected: hour 84) # Wasted GPU-hours (without FT) = GPUs × hours_worked_before_failure Wasted_GPU_hours = 1024 × 84 = 86,016 GPU-hours wasted per failure Wasted_cost = 86,016 × $3 = $258,048 wasted per failure # With 430 failures expected: effectively the job never completes

Step 3: GPU-hours wasted WITH fault tolerance (30-min checkpoints)

# Maximum work lost = work since last checkpoint = 30 minutes Checkpoint_interval = 0.5 hours Checkpoint_time = 0.05 hours (3 min) # Per failure: wasted compute = replay from checkpoint + checkpoint overhead Wasted_per_failure = 1024 GPUs × (0.5 + 0.05) hrs = 563 GPU-hours Cost_per_failure = 563 × $3 = $1,690 # Over full 168-hour run with 430 expected failures: Total_wasted = 430 × 563 = 242,090 GPU-hours wasted Total_wasted_cost = 430 × $1,690 = $726,700 over the 7-day run # vs. without FT: job never completes — effectively infinite cost # Checkpoint overhead (writing 30-min interval): 3 min / 30 min = 10% overhead # Tuning: use async checkpointing → reduce to <2% overhead # See Chapter 15 for checkpoint I/O optimization (async, distributed, incremental)

Conclusion: Without fault tolerance, a 1,024-GPU training job on a realistic cluster cannot complete a 7-day run — it will fail before reaching the end. With a checkpoint-requeue pipeline, the run completes with ~10% overhead from checkpoint writes and failure recovery, costing $726K in wasted GPU-hours over the full run. The engineering investment to build this pipeline (typically 1–2 weeks for a production-grade implementation) pays back in the first training run alone.

16.12 Profiling & Diagnostic Cookbook

When a 512-GPU Training Job is Slow — Systematic Diagnosis

Diagnostic Decision Tree

Step 1: Check MFU — compute achieved_TFLOPS / peak_TFLOPS. If <40%: scheduling or network issue. If 40–70%: likely communication overhead. If >70%: GPU-side issue (memory, kernel).

Step 2: Check AllReduce time — add NCCL timing: NCCL_TIMING=1. If AllReduce >15% of step time: topology placement issue or fabric congestion. Check sinfo --format=%f %n to verify nodes are on same ToR switches.

Step 3: Check stragglers — if AllReduce time is normal but throughput varies, one rank is slow. Use torch.distributed.monitored_barrier() to identify slow ranks. Check DCGM for thermal throttling on that node's GPUs.

Step 4: Check checkpoint I/O — if training pauses periodically, checkpoint writes are synchronous and blocking. Switch to async checkpointing (Torch Distributed Checkpoint) or reduce checkpoint frequency. See Chapter 15.

Step 5: Check gang start — run sacct -j $JOBID --format=JobID,Start --parsable2. If any rank started more than 5 seconds after the first: partial gang start, scheduler configuration issue.
# SLURM job diagnostic commands squeue -j $JOBID -o "%i %T %D %l %S %V %P %q %u" # Shows: job state, nodes, time limit, start time, submit time, partition, QOS, user sacct -j $JOBID --format=JobID,State,ExitCode,CPUTimeRAW,MaxRSS --parsable2 # Post-mortem: exit code, CPU time consumed, max memory used # Check fair-share balance (why is a job low priority?) sprio -l -j $JOBID # Shows: priority breakdown by component (age, fairshare, QOS, jobsize) # Check node topology placement scontrol show job $JOBID | grep NodeList srun --ntasks=1 bash -c "hostname; ibnetdiscover 2>/dev/null | grep $(hostname) | head -5" # Verify nodes are within expected switch domain # Kubernetes: GPU utilization across all pods in namespace kubectl top pods -n research --sort-by=cpu kubectl describe nodes | grep -A5 "Allocated resources"

Key Takeaways

Core Concepts
  • SLURM and Kubernetes solve different problems: SLURM excels at gang-scheduled, topology-aware HPC training; Kubernetes excels at containerized, auto-scaling inference serving. Most large organizations need both.
  • Gang scheduling is the non-negotiable constraint for distributed training: all ranks must start simultaneously, or NCCL initialization hangs and GPUs burn money doing nothing. SLURM provides this natively; Kubernetes requires Volcano or Kueue.
  • Topology-aware scheduling can recover 5–27 percentage points of MFU by ensuring TP groups stay within the NVLink domain (0.05 ms) and PP groups stay within the same ToR switch (0.15 ms), not scattered across spine switches (0.80 ms).
  • Fair-share scheduling automatically corrects cluster equity: teams that have used less of their allocation get boosted priority; over-consumers are deprioritized. The 14-day decay half-life means even heavy users return to fair standing over time.
Cost & Productivity Impact
  • Improving cluster utilization from 65%→85% on a 1,024-GPU cluster at $3/GPU-hr saves $614/hr = $5.4M/year — entirely from scheduling improvements, zero additional hardware.
  • A fault-tolerant checkpoint-requeue pipeline is mandatory at scale: on a 1,024-GPU cluster with 50-hour node MTBF, the first failure arrives within 23 minutes on average. Without checkpointing, a 7-day training run cannot complete. With 30-minute checkpointing, recovery cost per failure drops from hundreds of thousands to $1,690.
  • Multi-tenant GPU sharing via MIG on H100s allows 7 independent CI/CD evaluation jobs to run concurrently on a single GPU, recovering $0.43/hr from GPUs that would otherwise sit idle between training runs.
Principal-Level Insight
  • Silent Data Corruption (SDC) is the most dangerous failure mode: the GPU appears healthy by all hardware metrics (no ECC errors, nominal temperature) but produces wrong computation results. The only reliable detection is running known-correct "golden input" validation tests in the prolog before every large training job. No amount of post-hoc analysis recovers a multi-week training run corrupted by SDC.
  • The SLURM --switches constraint is the highest-ROI single configuration change for multi-node training: it prevents the scheduler from scattering a 512-GPU job across maximum fabric hops. The tradeoff — up to 30 minutes additional queue wait for better placement — pays back in reduced AllReduce latency for the entire multi-day run. At a step time of 1 second, 10% latency improvement = 0.1 GPU-seconds saved per step = hours of compute recovered on million-step runs.
  • Kubernetes PriorityClasses and SLURM QOS tiers solve different parts of the same problem: K8s priorities are about preemption order within a namespace; SLURM QOS is about preemption, resource caps, and scheduling boosters simultaneously. For a hybrid cluster, the policy must be consistent across both systems or teams will route jobs to whichever system gives them an advantage.
